SURVIVAL CHAOS  /  BOSS ENCOUNTER  /  T+600s  /  AS BUILT

LEVIATHAN

The model already had three separate weapon banks, and they happened to sit at the floor, the middle and the ceiling of the playable band. That was the entire fight, waiting to be wired as one. It is wired now — and this is what it does.

The arena, measured

Three facts decide everything else

You cannot outrun a bullet

Boss bullets orbit at 80°/s and its discs at 40°/s; the player at 16.3°/s. A bullet laps the world in 4.5 s and a disc in 9, the player in 22 s. Everything the boss fires comes back around and hits you in the back. Resogun calls this the Cylinder Wrap and treats it as a design rule. Here it was already true by accident, and the cadences below are set against it. The torpedoes are the exception that proves it: at 8.5 units a second they gain on a player who runs and never quite catch one, and they come back on purpose.

A shot never changes lane

Projectiles orbit with RotateAround, which preserves the distance from the axis they were born at — exactly, and for their whole life. The player is pinned to the lane at 19.72, and with both hitboxes a bullet can only touch them if it was born within about 0.23 of it, and a disc, which is 0.1 thick, within 0.19 — a little more than before 25 September, when the ship’s box grew a quarter. A shot fired off that lane misses forever. The muzzles span 18.72 to 20.75 across a hull 3.7 units deep, so this is the fact that decides how much of the boss’s fire is real. Easing a round onto the lane over its first moments recovered three quarters of it on 1 September. The torpedoes need none of it; they close on the lane themselves.

You cannot fly over it

The hull trigger is 19.1 units tall against a playable band of 10.5. It spans y −1.5–17.6; you live in y 2.8–13.3. It follows the band wherever the band goes, and stretches upright to reach a unit past both edges of one too tall for it. The boss is a moving wall — the Stormruler shape, already built. The only way past it is around, which makes the direction flip the most important key on the board.

The rig

Three banks, one third of the band each

The 32 enemyShootPivot transforms are not scattered. They cluster into three groups at three heights, and each group lands almost exactly on a third of the band the player can occupy. Nothing about the current fight knows this.

Before

It was a wall that sprays

All three banks fired every muzzle simultaneously, forever, on fixed 0.5 s and 3 s timers. No phases, no telegraph, no weak points, no reason to be at any particular height. The hull took damage anywhere, so the correct play was to hold the trigger and hope, and 300 HP was the only thing making it last. Everything below is the same 32 muzzles, fired differently.

As built

Armoured → Exposed → Scuttle

Three acts out of one 600-point pool, so the bar moves throughout and retuning an emplacement cannot quietly change what the boss is worth. The Resogun rule underneath is the one all five of its bosses share: the hull is not the target — specific, colour-coded things on it are, and destroying one takes an attack out of the fight. BossPhaseState holds the order with no Unity object attached, because “once, and never backwards” is the kind of rule that is impossible to check by playing.

1

ARMOURED — 240 HP across three weak points

The hull is invulnerable; bullets spark off it and die. Three weak points sit at the centroid of each bank, 80 HP each, and they are the only things that take damage. Killing one silences that bank for the rest of the fight’s two long acts and the arena gets measurably quieter.

The tension is geometric and comes free from the rig: player bullets travel at the player’s own height, so to kill a bank you have to fly at that bank’s height — which is precisely where its own fire is thickest. Each attack below is densest in its own third of the band.

They were neon green and they are red now, and the trade is worth stating even though it is settled. Green put the one thing the player must find as far from the lava as the wheel goes; red brought the whole of the boss’s output into one warm family, and buys separation from value and saturation instead of from hue. The cost was written down at the time: a red pod on an orange ship over orange lava is the harder read. The 11 September playtest found them every time, so they stay red.

Each pod sits at the centroid of its own bank’s muzzle heights, averaged from the model at build time rather than typed in, so it cannot drift away from the guns it stands for — world y 3.68, 8.41 and 12.83 on today’s band, each 0.875 in radius since the boss grew a quarter on 25 September. They are mounted proud of the hull, which is not decoration: the hull is a trigger that eats bullets, so a target inside it could never be hit at all.

Every volley is announced now. The lance always had its swell, and the ram its flash; the curtain and the crown, which are most of the act, arrived unannounced — and the playtest’s answer to a boss that read as scenery was clearer warnings rather than more damage. So a muzzle glows before it fires, in the thruster shader and the boss’s orange: the curtain lights every slot that will throw for 0.6 s and leaves its gap row dark, so the gap is visible before the wall leaves, and each glow goes out as its own disc does; the crown lights only the one muzzle about to fire, for 0.5 s. The glows are sized per bank — 0.9 across on the keel, 0.42 on the crown, whose rows were 0.4 to 0.52 apart and would run into one blob any wider — 0.5 to 0.65 since the boss grew, with the glows left as they were.

KeelCurtain 12 muzzles · one row open · discs · a disc every 2 s · interval 8 s

All twelve keel muzzles except one row, so each curtain has exactly one gap in it. The open row steps up one each volley and wraps, which makes it learnable rather than random. The rows are read off the muzzle heights rather than authored, because the second copy of a fact is the one that goes stale.

It is thrown now, and it leaves a disc at a time. The playtest note of 21 September was that the discs and the bullets ran round the ring in one lane. Each disc climbs or dives as it travels and comes off the floor and ceiling of the band the way a ball comes off a wall — the band the player’s own centre is clamped to, so hugging an edge is no escape. A row fans out steep up to steep down, at 4, 1.33, −1.33, −4 units a second, the same every curtain so each slot’s disc takes the same path and can be learned; a disc from a slot under the floor is always thrown up into the band. The eight discs leave 2 s apart — set by hand in the Inspector, as was the 8 s interval — so a curtain takes 14 s to empty, longer than its interval, and the attack has become a steady drip of discs rather than a wall.

It had never actually been a wall. The keel’s rows are 1.265 units apart and the darts it fired were 0.176 tall, so there were 1.09-unit gaps between every row — the one open row the whole design rests on was one of four ways through, and had been since the attack was built. The keel throws discs now, which is also what a bank firing out of slots rather than barrels should be throwing. At 0.7 units across they leave 0.615 units of clear gap against a ship 0.409 wide: passable, and only through the row that is meant to be open. The diameter is a difficulty dial rather than a look, and the first thing to lower if the curtain reads as unfair rather than as tight. Since the boss grew a quarter on 25 September the rows are 1.58 apart and the discs are still 0.7, so every gap is about 0.3 wider; discs 0.875 across would put the curtain back as it was.

CrownTorpedoes one every 3 s · 8.5 u/s · turn 90°/s · 10 s fuel

It was a rake — sixteen muzzles fired row by row, a staircase to dive under or climb over — and it ran beside the discs in the same lane. On 21 September it became one torpedo at a time: the sixteen muzzles taken in turn in the staircase’s old order, up the rows one pass and down the next, so a full pass is 48 s.

It flies like a torpedo, not a bullet with a lean. It leaves the muzzle at 3 units a second, runs straight for 0.3 s, spins up over 0.6 s to 8.5 — half again the player’s 5.6, since both moved on 22 September from 7.5 against 7 — and turns at no more than 90° a second, a turning circle about eleven units across. That is wider than the band, so it pulls out before the floor or ceiling at a tighter turn and arrives running along the edge; and coming about for a player behind it, it commits to over the top or underneath rather than dithering. It steers for 10 s and coasts for 2, half the size of the round it replaced, hit box and all, with the ship’s own thruster plume out of its tail that burns low out of the muzzle, full at cruise, and goes out with the fuel, so a spent torpedo looks spent. A torpedo that hits goes off.

The dodge is a delay. Its idea of where the player is catches up over about 0.6 s, and a hit is an intercept — the player’s box is 0.20 tall since 25 September, 0.16 when this was simulated, and a torpedo’s 0.09. At today’s speeds, simulated in TorpedoSteerTests rather than played: hold still and it hits; move two units a second and a half early and it follows you in; move them in the last 0.3 to 0.7 s and it passes where you were. Diving all the way to the floor is a timed escape now too — begun half a second to a second before it arrives it gets away, a second and a half early it follows you down, and 0.3 s is too late — where at 7.5 against 7 a dive was simply followed down. Run flat out along the ring and it gains, to about 2.7 behind after six and a half seconds, but never catches you, because it steers at where it last saw you. One that misses swings round and comes back while its fuel lasts, though with the player inside its turning circle it tends to circle past rather than connect.

ProwLance a laser · charge 1.2 s · out in 0.15 s · holds 0.5 s · cycle 2.6 s

The laser, timed rather than triggered. The prow pod swells for the whole 1.2 s wind-up — that swell is the telegraph, and it is the only one, because a muzzle is an empty transform with nothing to light up. Then the laser fires. The design said the lance captured your height during the charge; it does not. The boss holds altitude now, so what the lance actually does is own the middle of the band, which nothing else in the fight reaches. The 1.2 s telegraph was sized as one full band crossing at the player’s old 7 u/s. At 5.6 it was three quarters of one, and on the taller band of 25 September it is about two thirds; leaving the beam’s height takes about half a unit, so it is still always escapable, never for free. Since 21 September it is the whole of the warning — the laser is at full length a sixth of a second after it fires, so there is no watching it come. Shoot the pod off mid-charge and the warning stops with it, rather than leaving the player dodging a shot that is never coming — which now means cutting a sound as well as a swell, since the wind-up makes a noise. It rises 198 to 694 Hz over 1.15 s against the 1.2 s charge, and nothing in the sound packs was a charge: every engine and thruster measured as a flat drone, so both this and the ram’s were synthesised through the project’s own sfxr.

It fires nothing. Until 8 September the lance was forty rounds out of four muzzles, and the whole of the tuning was an attempt to make them read as one beam instead of as a stack. The measurement that ended that is worth keeping, because it says the attempt could never have worked: the ring is 86.2 units around and a round crossed it at 19.16 units a second, so rounds leaving 0.06 s apart were 1.15 units apart while each was 1.286 long. Consecutive rounds overlapped by 0.14 units before they had gone anywhere. It was already a solid line. It was just being drawn the expensive way — forty objects, forty colliders and forty lights for one continuous thing, into a light cluster that holds 24 per cell and drops the surplus in silence.

Welding them into four 3.3-unit rounds was tried first and rejected on sight: at that length they read as sticks flying in formation. 3.3 was not a taste either — it is the longest a straight mesh can be on a circle this size before it visibly stops following the lane, which is what said the shape wanted here was never a projectile at all. So from 8 September the lance was a single 12.8-unit arc, 24 segments bent along the ring, that grew from its own muzzle, slid round the band at the same 80°/s the rounds had, and lived two seconds.

In play it read as a giant bent log, not a laser — first on a flat material, then on one with a hot core and a fade, which helped the look and not the reading. The throw was the other half of it: a chunk that leaves the prow and slides away is a projectile, whatever it is drawn with. A laser stays attached to what fired it. So on 21 September it became one. After the charge it flashes out 160° round the ring in 0.15 s — the stretch the thrown arc used to cross — holds for half a second, and thins out over another 0.15, hurting no one as it does. It runs the way the boss is travelling when it fires, follows the prow’s bearing as the boss moves, and keeps the height it fired at, because that height is what the charge warned about. It is drawn as a thin white-hot core inside a soft orange glow: two ribbons turned to face the camera, the core opaque, the glow additive and writing its own motion vectors for FSR, so there is no face or edge to see from any angle.

Caught in it costs four hits at the far end and five by the prow, against about four for a pass of the thrown arc. It still bills on a 0.15 s tick and is still 0.2 wide, and it still takes its materials and its direction from the round prefab it replaced, so the weapon’s art lives in one place. Shoot the prow emplacement out while it is on and it goes out, as the charge already did.

2

EXPOSED — 270 HP on the core

All three weak points down. Every gun goes quiet, the boss holds a 2 s silence — every attack restarts its cadence from there, which is the beat the player is given to notice the fight changed shape — and the hull starts taking damage. This is Rotatron’s “it opens up” beat and Stormruler’s shed-armour beat in one moment. Anything mid-volley is stopped rather than allowed to finish: a lance still streaming out of an emplacement that was destroyed to end the phase is the exact thing the phase change is supposed to have stopped.

HullRam telegraph 1.0 s · charge 3.0 s · cycle 4.0 s, back to back

The hull flashes — by this act there are no emplacements left to light up — then it triples its orbit speed to 60°/s and sweeps around the ring at you. That is faster than the player’s 16.3°/s, so running the same way loses. The hull spans the whole band so climbing loses too. The counter is to dash straight through it — Doomsphere’s bull-fight, played as Resogun plays it. The dash was sized to it: 0.22 s of invincibility that covered 7.45 world units against a 7.05-unit hull, with the ship at 7.

Since 22 September a dash is shorter than the hull. The ship slowed by a fifth, so a dash covers about 6 units. The boss slowed with it for three days and the ram ran at 36°/s; on 25 September its cruise went to 20°/s, faster than the player, to make it chase, and the ram to 60. Met head-on the ram closes as well, so the two pass about 10.3 units through each other in the 0.22 s, against 8.5 at 36°/s and 10.7 with the ship at 7 — enough to clear the hull and the ship’s own width, with nearly nine tenths of the margin it had. A dash into a hull that is standing still still ends inside it. Worked out, not yet played.

And clearing it was never the rule. On 25 September the boss grew by a quarter, which made the hull 8.81 wide against a dash of about 6, and measuring that showed what the arithmetic above had assumed away: the hull hurts on first touch only, as the ship enters it, so a dash that is up at that moment carries the ship through however much hull is left. The bot flew the act against the bigger boss and met the hull 18 times; the 3 it dashed into cost nothing, twice with the ship still inside when the dash ran out, and the 15 it did not cost 16 hits. The width decides how long the ship is inside, not whether it is hit. On a band too tall for it the hull also stretches upright, model and box together, to reach a unit past the floor and ceiling, so the ram cannot be climbed over wherever the band is put.

Since 25 September it runs back to back, which is as often as it can. A ram is a 1 s charge plus a 3 s pass, and an attack whose running flag is still set is skipped — so the interval is 4, and anything under it would not fire more often, it would just stop meaning anything. The half second of hull that 4.5 left between passes is gone: the next charge starts as the last pass ends, so the flashing hull is the only rest. Checked in play: a pass every 4.0 s, 3.0 of them at three times cruise and 1.0 of charge. Against it the dash came back in 1.2 — until the same evening, when it went to one every ten seconds at your call, so it answers about two passes in five and the rest are flown round or taken.

It waits 3 s between turns, since 25 September. The boss turns round when the player is 20° past it, and faster than the player that meant a ram passing through them, turning 20° on and coming straight back — every half second to a second and a half, a hit every pass. The playtest bot, flown into this act with the balanced kit, took 24 hits and died in 21 s that way. With a turn cooldown of 3 s the boss carries on 80 to 130° past before it comes back; the same setup took 15 hits in 62 s and was still flying when the Scuttle began. It is EnemyMovement’s Turn Cooldown Seconds on the boss, 0 on every other enemy.

Then the dash went to ten seconds, and Slow Mo arrived to cover the gaps. The bot flew the act again from full health with the same kit and Slow Mo: 19 hits in 60.6 s, where the one-second dash had taken 15 in 62, and it killed the boss, with 7 health left — 5 dashes, 11 to 15 s apart, and Slow Mo twice, 30.4 s apart, each time with every route on the stick a predicted hit and no dash to take. A run that started the act on 13 health took 18 hits and died in the Scuttle at 55 s. About a quarter more hits a second, then; a bot, not a verdict.

ArenaWreckage a plate every 0.5 s · each lasts 11 s

Armoured is a fight about height; Exposed threw that away, because the hull spans the whole band and any altitude is as good as another. Wreckage puts the three heights back with their meaning reversed: the boss sheds plates of its own hull from the emplacements the player themselves destroyed, at those emplacements’ own heights. The places worth flying to become the places worth avoiding, and the player already knows where they are.

They do not drift. Everything else here rides the ring; these are the one thing that does not, which is what makes them read as debris rather than as another attacker — and it means the boss, cruising at 20°/s while it sheds, leaves a wake of its own hull and eventually comes back round into it. Life over cadence is the tuning: 11 s against 0.5 holds about twenty-two plates, seven at any one height, which against a 22 s lap is a forced move about every three seconds — 20 to 21 were standing when it was checked in play on 25 September. It was 0.7 and about sixteen plates until that day, and before that 16 over 1.8 — about nine plates — but that density only exists in a steady state that takes a whole plate lifetime to build, and the act used to be over before it arrived, so the number it was tuned for had never once been on screen. Three shots break one. It earns no experience — clearing a lane is most of its reward — but since 22 September a broken plate counts as a wreck for salvage: when the player is hurt and a piece is due, it leaves a green +1 where it was, which is what keeps healing alive once the waves have stopped. Which pod sheds next counts over the wrecked ones rather than indexing all three and skipping, so the order the player killed them in cannot change how much wreckage they face.

3

SCUTTLE — last 90 HP

Every magazine on the ship cooks off at once: all 32 muzzles fire simultaneous volleys on a 0.8 s cycle — 1.2 until 25 September — regardless of which emplacements are wrecked, until it dies. Loud, short, and survivable if you keep moving. It exists so the kill has a crescendo instead of a health bar quietly reaching zero. Plates already in the air ride into it on their own clocks, so the last act starts inside the mess the second one made.

Built & verified

Space, or the right shoulder / south button on a pad. A committed burst along the heading captured at the moment it starts, invincible for its whole length — once every ten seconds, since 25 September.

Why it lives on the movement, not the ship

The ship and the Main Camera run the same PlayerMovement component at the same authored speed, and that identity is the whole of how the camera keeps station. A dash applied to the ship alone flings it out of frame five times faster than the camera can follow — the same bug as an unshared speed pick, arriving in a fifth of a second instead of over a run. So the burst is shared static state, and the measured drift is zero rather than small.

The heading is captured, not read

Taken once, when the burst begins, and normalised. Letting go halfway through does not strand the ship and grabbing a new direction halfway through does not steer it. It also gives the burst a direction on the frames where nothing is held, where it falls back to the way the nose is pointing — so a dash always goes somewhere.

Player side

Ways to defeat it

Choose your remaining fight

Three weak points means three opening moves, and the order is the difficulty dial the player sets themselves. Kill the Prow first and you remove the only attack that punishes standing still — the safe opening. Kill the Crown first and nothing hunts you for the rest of the phase. Kill the Keel first and the discs stop, but you spend the whole fight dodging torpedoes.

A verb that arrives late

Everything the ship could do before the dash was continuous — orbit, climb, flip — so every threat was answered by having started moving earlier, and none could be answered now. That is why nothing in the fight was worth telegraphing. The dash is the answer that arrives late, and it is what makes a 1.2 s Lance charge a decision rather than a countdown.

Since 25 September it arrives every ten seconds, and Slow Mo is the other late answer: a pick from level 8, in Piercing Rounds’ old place, that slows the whole game to 0.4 for five real seconds on E, or Y or the left trigger, once every 30 — 24 and 18 with the next two picks. It was built at three; you set five the same evening, and a change to its length in the Inspector now takes effect at once, even mid-slowdown. Everything slows, the ship with it, so what it buys is time to see, not speed.

Upgrades read on the boss

A pod is 1.75 units across since the boss grew a quarter, and the sextuple spread is about a unit tall, so a centred volley lands all six and one aimed a unit off lands two. At stage 0 and 0.5 s between volleys that is a long minute per emplacement; fully upgraded it is a handful of seconds. The boss is where twenty picks of Attack Speed and Shot Upgrade finally show up as something other than a bigger number.

The bar stays honest

600 total: 80 apiece on the three emplacements, then 270 on the open hull, then the last 90 to the Scuttle. One pool, so the bar moves throughout Armoured rather than sitting full while the player does real work — and so retuning a pod cannot quietly change what the boss is worth.

It was 300, with 50 a pod, and that is why the fight played easy: a run ends at forty damage a second, so the armoured act was about six seconds — two cycles of attacks on four, five and six second intervals. The cadences never got to say anything. Health decides how many cycles the player sees; cadence decides how hard each is. Both moved, then the fight came back reported as good but a bit long and the cut landed entirely on the first act, which was half the bar on its own and is also the act you must fly between three heights to finish.

Played again at that shape it still ran long, and this time the report was about the fight’s length rather than any one act of it — so the second cut came off the pool as a whole rather than off a phase: 750 to 600, pods from 100 to 80, the open hull from 360 to 270, and the Scuttle left alone at 90. The Scuttle is the one number here that is a duration rather than a fraction — it is a crescendo measured in seconds, and shrinking it with the rest of the bar would have shortened the only part of the fight that was never the problem.

Found while measuring

Thirteen things the build turned up

Every one of them but the crown was invisible from the outside, and none of those would have been found by playing.

The guns did not turn with the ship — fixed

EnemySpaceShip rotates the NAVEBOSS model ±90° to face its direction of travel, but all 32 pivots hung off the Boss root, which only ever faces the middle of the arena. So when RingChase reversed the boss, the ship turned and the guns stayed put — the prow muzzle swinging 8.7 world units. A telegraphed lance is meaningless if the muzzle is not where the barrel is, so this blocked everything else. The muzzles hang off a rig carrying the same EnemySpaceShip component now — not a second copy of the rule kept in step with the first, the same rule read once in the same frame. Every muzzle’s position in the boss’s own frame is identical to three decimals; mirroring is all it adds.

Three quarters of the boss’s fire could not reach the player — fixed, twice

A shot keeps the radius it was born at, forever. On the lane as it was on 1 September the muzzles were spread across a hull 3 units deep and sat from 13.16 to 15.02, against a hittable lane of 13.39–14.05. Only 8 of the 32 were inside it — keel 3 of 12, prow 1 of 4, crown 4 of 16 — so a curtain of twelve arrived as a wall of three, the rake had four of its sixteen steps, and the lance fired one muzzle in four. The fight’s own vocabulary was describing something the player never faced.

The muzzles do not move, because that is the one thing the rig must not do. The shot leaves the barrel and then eases onto the lane, radius only, through the same easing every ship already uses on its way to the ring. It is off unless asked for, so the player’s own rounds are untouched by construction. Measured after: 87–100% of boss fire in the lane, against about a quarter before.

On 21 September the easing went to 0 on both disc prefabs and on the four bullet prefabs the boss fires, and nothing in the code asked for it. On the 18.72 lane a disc, 0.1 thick, has to be born within 0.17 of the lane to touch the player, and no keel muzzle is — so for one build, 26_0921-1625_camera, the curtain could not hit anyone, and most of the Scuttle’s rounds missed the same way. It went back to 5 the same afternoon. Nothing had noticed: no test read the value off the prefabs, and a curtain that cannot hit still looks like a curtain. One does now, off every round the boss fires, and fails naming any that is not at 5.

The emplacements swapped lanes with the heading — fixed

A pod is mounted proud of the hull along the tangent, and the arena is a circle, so 3.95 units out from a point on a 13.7-unit ring leaves you 0.56 units outside it. The builder takes that back off — but the correction was authored on the mirroring rig, so it mirrored too, and in one heading it was added outward instead of subtracted inward. That does not cancel the error, it doubles it: 13.73 travelling one way, 14.80 the other. It is re-applied every frame against the direction the middle of the arena is actually in now. The mirroring that was wanted survives — the keel pod sits at +0.395 and −0.395 along the ring, and at +0.056 inward in both.

Wreckage billed a collision many times over — fixed

The first guess was the plate’s shape, on the theory that a tumbling box sweeps the player in and out of itself. The collider became a sphere and it changed nothing. What turns is the player: the ship is aimed at the arena’s axis every frame and rolled by SpaceShipPitch, so a ship sitting inside a stationary trigger keeps carrying its own collider back out through the surface and in again, and every one of those is a fresh OnTriggerEnter. With the arena verifiably empty, one plate took a stationary player from 19 hit points to 0 in 0.64 s. A plate goes intangible while the player is inside it now; the same test costs exactly one hit.

Two silent races in the emitter — fixed

BossEmitter resolved the health bar once, in Start, and Timer switches that bar on at the same second the spawn stream releases the boss. A tag search does not see inactive objects, so whichever ran first decided whether the boss had a working bar for the whole fight — and the failure is silent, because the fight plays correctly and the bar just sits full. And HitFlash claimed every renderer beneath it, which would have meant a shot breaking on the armour lighting the three pods as brightly as a shot that hit one. That distinction is the whole of the first act.

Every boss round was lit from below — fixed

The bullet lights were placed on each projectile’s transform. That is the same point as the mesh on the player’s shots and is not on the boss’s: its imported dart art sits 0.266 units above its own pivot, so every boss round lit the empty air beneath itself while the round stayed dark. The pool had been written and tuned against the one case where the two coincide. The colliders were correct throughout, which is why nothing about it read as a hitbox problem. Each projectile measures the centre of what it actually draws now, once, and the error is 0.000.

The guns fired from mid-air — fixed

The keel’s and crown’s 28 muzzles came over from the old boss script a full unit in front of the hull — 0.98 to 1.45 — so every disc and round appeared out of thin air beside the ship rather than leaving it. That was invisible while nothing lit the muzzles; the moment they glowed, the glows hung in space. They sit 0.05 in front of their slots now, measured off the hull mesh with a ray test rather than by opening the prefab, and a second pass moves nothing.

Two discs left as one — fixed

The keel’s slots come in pairs 0.27 apart against a disc 0.7 across, so a pair released together left as one double disc, and a row thrown at one angle still left as one stack of four, because they shared a height and eased onto one lane. Seen in play both times. The curtain releases a disc at a time now, and each slot of a row is thrown at its own angle.

Torpedoes passed over the player they hit — fixed

The first steering torpedoes flew their pivot into the player’s, and every one that reached a still player in play counted as a hit by distance. By the boxes it was a miss: the dart’s box hangs 0.27 above its pivot with the art, and the player’s is 0.16 tall, so it passed clean over. A torpedo aims its box at the player’s box now, measured every frame because the box swings with the roll — and 29 of 41 met a still player’s box in the check that followed. Enemy rounds also fly on through the player rather than stopping, which is harmless for a round that never comes back and was a double hit waiting for one that does; a torpedo goes off.

The crown rose out of reach — fixed

On 21 September the player’s bounds moved down 1.7, from a band of 4.42–13.32 to 2.72–11.62, and the boss’s spawn stayed at 7.79. Its banks are fixed to the boss, so they did not follow: the crown emplacement, at 12.71 with a 0.7 radius, bottomed out at 12.01 against a ceiling of 11.62. Rounds leave the ship 0.045 below its centre, so only the six-shot upgrade’s top round, flown at the ceiling, reached it at all — and the hull stays armoured while any emplacement stands, so in most runs the boss could not be killed. The lance had drifted to the upper part of the band too, 2 above its middle. The boss spawned 1.7 lower from then, at 6.09, which put every bank back where it sits against the band; the spawn check had passed throughout, because the boss still appeared inside the band — it was the guns that left. Found before the build, by measuring the banks against the new band. Since 25 September it cannot happen again: the boss takes its height from the band as it arrives, 1.35 under its middle, and BossBandHeightTests holds every emplacement inside the band for bands moved up and down.

The lance fired out of the tail — fixed

The beam took its direction from the round it replaced, and it took it once: when the beam was built, on the first lance of the fight, from the round for whichever way the boss was travelling then. The boss turns round every time the player crosses it, and the prow turns with it, so from the first turn on every lance fired the other way was a beam leaving the prow backwards — across the boss’s own hull and away from the player it was chasing. The thrown arc before it read its speed the same way, so this had been true since 8 September; it was seen in play on 25 September, with a faster boss turning more. The beam reads the direction from the round on every shot now. BossLanceBeamTests fires one beam left, right and left again and checks each, and two playtest-bot runs the same day logged 41 lances, the first before a turn and the rest after it, every one leaving the front of the ship the way it was travelling.

The dash never had to clear the hull — measured

Everything written about the ram’s counter, here and in the code, sized the dash against the hull’s width: 7.45 units of invincible travel against 7.05, then about 6 against 7.05 with the ram closing head-on to make up the rest. Growing the boss to 8.81 wide on 25 September looked as if it would close that for good, so it was measured before anything was retuned. The hull hurts through Player.OnTriggerEnter, which fires once, when the ship first touches it: a dash that is up at that moment carries the ship through whatever is left, and the bot’s did exactly that, twice ending with the ship still inside and nothing landing. The width was never the thing to size against.

The orphaned trigger volume — deleted

27b8a93 added a “Laser Trigger” sphere to the prefab; 2d44065 deleted ColliderScript_3, the only thing that read it. The GameObject sat on the prefab carrying a broken MonoBehaviour reference. It is gone.

Scope

What is deliberately not taken from Resogun

  • Voxel shatter. The hull is a single four-material mesh, not voxels. Destroyed emplacements go dark and drop wreckage rather than exploding into cubes.
  • Rotatron’s interior phase. The model is solid and the arena is a ring the boss sits on, not a cylinder it forms. There is nothing to fly inside.
  • Instant-kill hitboxes. This game has a health bar and twenty levels of upgrades built around surviving hits. Contact costs one hit point, as it does now.
  • Serpentinus. A segmented body that slithers round the ring is a second boss, not a phase of this one.
Still open

What has not been settled

The plates read only as a silhouette

They carry the boss’s own hull material, which is nearly black. Against the sky or the lava they are unmistakable; against the dark island one can be lost. An emissive tint would fix it, and would also make them the first thing in the arena that glows because it is dangerous rather than because it is hot. It was put as a question and answered — no glow for now — so this is a deliberate no rather than an open one, worth revisiting only if a run shows a plate actually being lost.

The red pods read — settled

The comment written when they turned red flagged this as the first thing to question if the armoured act became hard to aim at, and a later pass moved every hostile thing off orange before being reverted for washing a third of the island green. The 11 September playtest answered it: the pods were found every time. If one is ever lost over the lava, the cause is intensity rather than hue — their cores blow out to the same white-yellow the lava does — so the lever is their brightness, not their colour.

Whether the laser reads as a laser

The thrown arc read as a log, and the laser that replaced it on 21 September has had no verdict yet. The things to watch for are its own: whether the flash-out reads as firing rather than as switching on, whether half a second of hold is long enough to register and short enough not to feel like a wall, and whether the charge alone is warning enough now that the beam cannot be watched coming. Where it runs straight at the camera it pinches to a glint, which is what any camera-facing beam does. Reach, widths, the three timings and the tick are all constants in one file, which is the same reason the numbers it replaced were easy to move.

The torpedoes at one every 3 seconds

Played against on 21 September, twice. At one a second, 13 of 18 hit and the hull was gone in 46 s. At one every 2 s with a 12 s life, 11 of 33 hit and 14 of 20 hull went in 67 s — and most of those hits came from torpedoes over 4 s old, coming round again. Since then the interval is 3 s, the cruise 7.5 rather than 8, and the torpedo half the size; the smaller box makes it an eighth-of-a-unit intercept, easier to slip. That shape is in 26_0921-1757_laser. On 22 September, at your call, the cruise went up to 8.5 while the player slowed to 5.6, which is in 26_0924-0845_salvage and has not been played yet. If the old hunters feel cheap, the fuel is the dial: 6 s would keep the second pass and lose most of the circling.

Which camera the fight is read through — settled

Nearly every attack here is dodged by reading heights, and the close camera draws something ten units round the ring two thirds its size and never shows the whole band at once. Settled on 24 September by playing: the whole band from 10 out, holding the band’s middle. Since 25 September its lens fits the band’s live height and keeps the top and bottom 8% of the screen clear, so a ship on the ceiling is drawn below the countdown and the boss’s bar — about 70° on today’s 10.5 band, where it was 54° on 8.9. The other six presets stay on the debug menu (F8, then 9).

The fight against a slower ship

Everything here was tuned against a player at 7, and on 22 September the player went to 5.6 while the boss’s rounds kept their speeds. Worked out above and not yet played: the lance’s charge is three quarters of a band crossing, a dash covers about 6 units of a hull 8.81 wide since the boss grew, which turned out not to matter, since the hull hurts only on first touch; and a vertical dash carries 3.6 of the 4.4 to 4.7 between banks. If the ram starts to feel like a wall, the dash’s speed multiplier is the dial: 6.25 would put a dash back where it was. It is all in 26_0925-2101_bigShips, with the debug menu.

A more aggressive boss

Asked for on 25 September, and three of the four steps offered were taken; then, played, the boss was still slow, so it went on to 20°/s. That is faster than the player’s 16.3, so it catches a ship that only runs, and it takes three Move Speed picks to outrun it — two did until you moved the lane out to 19.72 the same evening, since the ship’s speed is set in units a second and the boss’s in degrees. It stays on you, the lance’s 160° and its plates reach you more, and the ram rises with it to 60. The ram runs back to back, a plate sheds every 0.5 s, and the Scuttle fires every 0.8. The first act’s cadences were left as you tuned them. And it waits 3 s between turns, so a ram that passes you carries on rather than sawing back over you.

The playtest bot flew it twice that day, without god mode. With the balanced boss kit, two Move Speed picks, it stayed just ahead of the boss, took two hits in 136 s, and had the prow and crown down in 43 s; then it sat on the floor a unit under the keel pod for 45 s, which is its own height choice rather than the fight. At base speed and level 1 it dashed 28 times to stay 24–50° ahead and took no hits in 105 s. So chased, the bot was never caught. A bot sees more than a player and acts on it, so this is a reading, not a verdict. If it is too much, or not enough, each is one number on the Boss prefab: EnemyMovement’s rotation speed, and the intervals of Hull - Ram, Hull - Wreckage and Scuttle.

The same evening the dash went from every second to every ten, which makes it the answer to about two rams in five rather than to each one; the bot’s runs with it are under the Ram. If that is the part that is too much, the dial is PlayerDash’s cooldown on the player, not anything on the boss.

A boss a quarter bigger

At your call on 25 September the boss grew by a quarter with the player and the four enemies, its weak points and guns with it, and you grew the band to 10.5 so the banks still land on its floor, middle and ceiling: the crown sits 0.46 under the ceiling, as it did before. The bot took all three weak points inside the band. Not all of it shows: the camera widens with the taller band, so on screen everything is only about 5% bigger than it was; the curtain’s gaps are about 0.3 wider, since the discs did not grow; and pods 1.75 across are easier to hit. The boss needs a band of about 9.6 — any shorter and the crown leaves it again. It is in 26_0925-2101_bigShips, not yet played.

Healing in the fight

Salvage replaced the level-up health drop on 22 September: while the player is hurt, a wreck can leave a green piece worth one hit point, more often the more is missing. In the fight the wrecks are stragglers from the waves, which live up to two minutes after the boss arrives, and broken hull plates. Emplacements drop nothing, because the boss chases the player round the ring and would run over scrap left at its own face. Not yet played.

A bot reached it twice

The playtest bot’s first full run on 22 September arrived at level 20 with 13 health, took the prow, crown and keel at 37, 38 and 41 s, and died 11 s into Exposed — 15 hits in 51 s, 12 of them in Armoured. That was before salvage, and before a review found the bot had never seen two of the four enemy kinds, only their rounds.

The run after the fix saw everything, took 7 hits in its first eight minutes and 36 in the two before the boss, arrived with 11 health and died 8 s into Armoured, the crown at 70 of 80. So the waves’ last two minutes, not the fight, did the damage. A bot sees a quarter of a second late and knows more than a player does: data points, not a verdict.
